Minimum Viable Second Property Selection Doctrine

Core belief

A good property can still be the wrong property for your plan.

Selection promise

We determine what the next property must achieve, reject the options that fail the plan and research what remains before producing the shortlist.

Simple public process

Define the Job → Reject What Fails → Research What Remains

Do not create another large branded framework.

This can sit inside Move 4 of the Five Moves, whose current approved mechanism already includes property selection, exit thinking and downside survivability.

Doctrine Principle 1 — The property gets a job before it gets a name

Every property must have a defined purpose.

Examples:

family home;

rental asset;

capital-growth asset;

future retirement option;

bridge to another purchase;

preserve one spouse’s future buying capacity;

provide rental fallback;

support a later trade-up.

Marketing lines:

Your next property needs a job description before it needs an address.

Before asking which property to buy, decide what the property must do.

The right project depends on the job it must perform in your plan.

Doctrine Principle 2 — Reject before recommending

Do not begin with a shortlist of attractive options.

Begin with the disqualification rules.

Possible rejection reasons:

requires too much cash;

monthly commitment is too high;

consumes too much future borrowing capacity;

exit price may become difficult;

future buyer group is too narrow;

too much competing supply;

rental demand is weak;

layout does not match likely future buyers;

depends too heavily on future transformation;

prevents the intended next move.

Marketing lines:

Our shortlist begins with a rejection list.

Before we show what may work, we identify what cannot work for your plan.

Most properties do not need to be bad to be rejected. They only need to be wrong for your objective.

Doctrine Principle 3 — Entry price must be defensible

“Good entry price” needs a clearer meaning.

It is not merely:

lower PSF than another launch;

early-bird discount;

developer promotion;

cheaper than the next phase.

It means the purchase can be defended against:

comparable resale properties;

neighbouring projects;

future competing supply;

what the eventual buyer can afford;

realistic rental income;

expected holding period.

Marketing line:

A discount does not automatically make the entry price safe. The future buyer still has to justify paying more.

Doctrine Principle 4 — Buy with the future buyer in mind

This is one of your strongest existing beliefs.

Before buying, define:

who is likely to buy later;

why they would choose this property;

what income they may need;

what competing options they will have;

whether the future price remains financeable;

whether the layout and property type match their needs.

Marketing lines:

Before buying, we ask who is likely to buy it from you later.

You are not only choosing a property. You are choosing your future buyer.

A strong exit begins with a clear buyer—not with hope that prices will rise.

Doctrine Principle 5 — Rentability is the backup plan

Do not market rentability only as yield.

It also provides resilience when:

plans change;

the owner moves elsewhere;

sale timing is poor;

the property must be held longer;

income temporarily changes.

Questions:

Is there a broad tenant pool?

Is the unit size suitable?

Is rent dependent on one employer or location?

How much competing rental supply exists?

What is realistic net rent after costs?

Would the rent meaningfully support the mortgage?

Marketing line:

Rentability matters not only because you want rental income. It matters because life may not follow the original plan.

Doctrine Principle 6 — It must survive difficult periods

The property should remain manageable through:

higher interest rates;

vacancy;

job changes;

family expenses;

delayed sale;

weaker market sentiment;

temporary single-income periods.

Marketing line:

A property is not suitable only because you can buy it today. It must remain workable when life becomes less convenient.

This preserves your Exit Before Entry and Exit-Safe thinking without making those names the main marketing category.

Doctrine Principle 7 — The next purchase must preserve the move after it

This is the most promising differentiator.

Ask:

How much borrowing ability remains?

How much cash and CPF remain?

Are both names tied up?

Can the property be sold at the required future time?

Does the monthly commitment remove future flexibility?

What happens if the second-property plan changes?

Does the purchase create options or remove them?

Marketing lines:

The second property should not become the reason there is no third move.

We judge the next property partly by what it leaves you able to do afterwards.

A purchase can look profitable and still leave the family structurally stuck.

Three client examples

Example 1 — Decoupled couple

Their initial desire:

Buy an investment property using the freed name.

The property’s actual job:

produce reasonable rental support;

appeal to a broad future buyer group;

remain within a defensible quantum;

avoid consuming all borrowing capacity;

allow an own-stay move later.

Possible automatic rejections:

niche one-bedroom units;

high investor concentration;

excessive future supply;

price dependent on speculative transformation;

quantum that blocks the later family-home purchase.

Example 2 — HDB family moving into private property

Their initial desire:

Sell HDB and upgrade.

The property’s actual job:

support family lifestyle;

remain affordable through children’s growing expenses;

hold value across the expected holding period;

appeal to the next family buyer;

preserve the possibility of a later investment property.

Possible automatic rejections:

attractive but undersized units;

excessive monthly commitment;

weak school/family buyer demand;

high exit quantum;

limited fallback rental demand.

Example 3 — Existing condo owner

Their initial desire:

Keep the condo and buy another.

The property’s actual job:

diversify rather than duplicate current exposure;

provide a different buyer or tenant group;

avoid overconcentration in one district or property type;

remain manageable if one unit is vacant;

preserve sufficient reserves.

Possible automatic rejections:

two assets exposed to the same tenant market;

both depending on the same transformation story;

total monthly commitment too dependent on perfect occupancy;

weak future buyer affordability.

Marketing demonstrations we should build from the doctrine

Demonstration 1 — Rejected Property Case

Structure:

What the client originally wanted

What property looked attractive

Why it appeared suitable

Why you rejected it

What condition it failed

What route was eventually taken

What the final choice preserved

This should become a repeated content series.

Possible title:

Why We Rejected the Property Our Client Originally Wanted

Demonstration 2 — The Property Job

Show an anonymised one-page brief:

The next property must:

remain below $X commitment;

appeal to a family resale buyer;

have a rental fallback;

preserve $X liquidity;

not block the later purchase;

remain workable for at least X years.

Possible title:

Before We Shortlist Properties, We Write This One Page

Demonstration 3 — Good Property, Wrong Client

Take one recognised project and explain:

“This may be a good property—but it would fail this particular household’s plan because…”

This allows you to discuss projects without claiming they are universally good or bad.

Possible title:

A Good Project Can Still Be the Wrong Move

Demonstration 4 — Three Similar Properties, Three Different Jobs

Show how:

Property A suits rental;

Property B suits family ownership;

Property C better preserves the next move.

Possible title:

Same Budget. Three Properties. Completely Different Jobs.

Demonstration 5 — The Rejection List

Show five reasons a property never entered the shortlist.

Possible title:

Five Reasons We Remove a Property Before the Client Ever Views It
