The doctrine needs a decision hierarchy—not one giant checklist

I recommend three levels.

Level 1 — Non-negotiable rejection gates

A property is rejected when it fails one of these, regardless of popularity or attractive features.

A. It breaks the household route

Examples:

wrong ownership name;

consumes a name required for the following move;

prevents the intended hold-two structure;

requires selling something the family intended to preserve;

conflicts with the client’s actual timeline;

cannot serve the role assigned to it.

B. It is not safely affordable through the intended period

Not merely “the bank will lend.”

Consider:

monthly payment;

cash and CPF remaining;

one-income stress;

interest-rate stress;

vacancy;

children or family expenses;

completion-stage cashflow;

renovation and transaction costs;

ability to hold longer than expected.

C. The exit audience is too weak

Examples:

too few realistic future buyers;

future quantum exceeds likely buyer affordability;

niche unit type;

investor-heavy exit with few owner-occupiers;

too little transaction volume;

difficult layout;

weak reasons to choose it over alternatives.

D. It requires the wrong holding period

Examples:

client may need liquidity in five years but the property needs eight to ten;

major transformation happens after the intended exit;

surrounding supply peaks when the client expects to sell;

the property reaches an unattractive age or lease point at exit.

E. It removes the next move

Examples:

too much loan used;

insufficient reserves remaining;

both names locked;

too much dependency on rental;

future sale becomes essential instead of optional;

the next planned purchase becomes financially impossible.

These five are the real backbone.

A project that fails here should not be rescued by an MRT, nice view, popular developer or impressive showroom.

Level 2 — Investment and market-strength filters

Only properties that pass Level 1 are compared here.

transformation and demand growth;

price logic;

supply risk;

future buyer affordability;

rentability;

transaction liquidity;

competing projects;

development quality;

lease and age;

completion timing.

Level 3 — Preference and unit optimisation

These matter, but should not disguise a weak project.

floor;

view;

facing;

balcony;

study;

extra toilet;

larger common room;

facilities;

branded developer;

integrated development;

proximity premium;

high-floor premium.

The question is not:

“Is this feature good?”

It is:

“Will the client or future buyer recover enough value from the premium paid?”

Your trade-off decisions

I would not automatically allow transformation to win.

A strong master plan can still produce a weak investment when:

the premium has already been priced in;

transformation takes too long;

many future projects share the same benefit;

new supply absorbs the incoming demand;

the future buyer can purchase newer alternatives nearby;

the current entry price assumes perfect execution.

The correct rule is:

Transformation is valuable only when the demand benefit is not already fully priced in.

Evaluate every transformation through five questions:

Is it committed or merely proposed?

When will it materially affect demand?

Who exactly will move, work, study or spend there?

How much additional supply arrives with it?

How much of the story is already inside today’s price?

Strong rentability but narrow exit audience

You chose exit audience. I agree.

Rent can provide holding support, but it cannot fully compensate for:

poor resale liquidity;

narrow future buyer demand;

an exit quantum few buyers can finance;

oversupply of similar rental units.

Rule:

Rental can support the holding period. It cannot replace a credible exit.

Great family property but reduces future borrowing ability

You chose the family property.

This cannot be an automatic rule.

It depends on the agreed route.

If the client’s priority is:

“We need the best long-term family home, and a second property is secondary,”

then the family property may correctly win.

But if the agreed mandate is:

“Own a suitable home while preserving the ability to acquire another property,”

then excessive borrowing is a strategy failure.

Rule:

Lifestyle can win only when the client knowingly accepts the opportunity cost.

The recommendation should show:

what they gain;

how much future capacity is lost;

which future move becomes harder;

whether that trade-off is intentional.

Good value but wrong ownership structure

You appeared to favour good value.

I disagree strongly.

A good-value property in the wrong structure can:

trigger unnecessary ABSD;

lock the wrong name;

remove future borrowing flexibility;

prevent the next purchase;

force an earlier sale;

create legal and tax complications.

Rule:

Structure determines whether the opportunity is suitable. Value only matters after the structure works.

A cheap wrong move is still a wrong move.

Not just “new MRT.”

How many minutes are genuinely saved?

retail;

waterfront;

community facilities;

low-density land becoming higher-density;

ageing stock being replaced;

limited future plots;

new GLS pipeline;

competing private supply;

Transformation that adds demand and excessive competing supply may not improve resale performance.

Classify each catalyst:

Announced with timeline

Marketing speculation
