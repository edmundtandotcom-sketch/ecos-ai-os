I'm here because "genuinely strong project" and "right for you, in the right unit, at the right time" are completely different questions. And that's where people get burned — not because the project is bad, but because nobody told them what to actually check.

## SCENE 4 — WHAT NOBODY'S SHOWING YOU (4:30–17:00)

[PUNCH: "15 things I found — some on the site, some off it"]

Let me walk you through what I actually found. I'm grouping these into six clusters, because that's how they show up in real life — not as fifteen random complaints, but as patterns, some physical, some about timing, that repeat depending on which stack and which year you're looking at.

### Cluster A — Getting in, getting out

Thomson Reserve has two access points, and they're not interchangeable. The right side is the guest and resident entrance. The left side is where residents actually drive in. [PUNCH: "Right = guest/resident entry · Left = resident vehicle entrance"] That split sounds minor until you actually walk it — because here's what the site plan doesn't tell you until you're standing on it: this is a long, narrow site. Block to block, some of the spans run past 240 metres. [PUNCH: "up to 240m block-to-block"] If you're not staying near the main gateway, driving to your own drop-off point is genuinely faster than walking there. I confirmed this myself standing at both ends.

And then there's something I don't think anyone else is going to tell you, because you only find it by actually standing at the far gates during peak hour: there are side gates at each far end of the development. If you're calling a Grab from one of those ends, you are not meeting your driver at your doorstep — you are walking, sometimes running, to reach the pickup point before the fare adjusts or the driver cancels. The sharpest version of this sits right by Thomson Plaza, where pickup and drop-off already gets packed on its own. [ENGAGE: "Have you ever lost a Grab booking because you couldn't reach the pickup point in time? Tell me in the comments."] That's not a developer problem. That's just physics on a site this size. But it's exactly the kind of thing that never shows up in a sales brochure, and it's exactly why we walk the site before we let a client commit.

### Cluster B — Getting up

Here's one that's pure numbers, no opinion involved. Blocks 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 each carry 9 stacks. Block 11 carries 10. [PUNCH: "9–10 stacks per block"] And every residential block has three lifts. [PUNCH: "3 lifts per block"]

Do the math with me. Nine or ten stacks, each running up to 21 or even 30 storeys, sharing three lifts. [ENGAGE: "If you've lived in a block with this kind of stack-to-lift ratio before, how bad was peak hour, really?"] At 7:30 in the morning, when every household on every one of those floors is trying to leave for work or school at the same time — that is a real bottleneck, and it's one you can actually predict and avoid if you know which blocks and which stacks to look at before you buy.

### Cluster C — The daily friction

This next one isn't just me. Morning and evening, the stretch of road around this site genuinely jams — and that's not only my own site-walk timing, that's what residents who actually live in the area will tell you if you ask them directly. Lunch and dinner, same story. Sunday morning, without fail, every single time I've been there — that part is my own repeated observation, so take it as exactly that.

Independent reviews of this project already flag "traffic sensitivity around Bright Hill Drive" as a known trade-off of a development this size — 1,268 units is a lot of car trips concentrated into the same few minutes, every single day. [PUNCH: "1,268 units, same roads, same peak minutes"] And here's the part that reframes the whole MRT story: the Upper Thomson MRT connection isn't just a nice-to-have on the marketing deck. It exists specifically to take pressure off this exact chokepoint. The two-minute walk to the station matters more here than at most projects, because it's the actual release valve for a jam that's already there today.

And here's the part that actually matters for your decision: don't expect this to get fixed by more road capacity. There is work happening on Sin Ming Avenue right now — a dedicated bus lane, new cycling paths, wider footpaths, completing around 2029. [PUNCH: "LTA Transit Priority Corridor, completing ~2029"] But that's an upgrade for buses, bikes and walking. It is not more lanes for cars. And it can't really be, because one side of this site is boxed in by the MRT viaduct, and the other side backs onto Thomson Nature Park and the Central Catchment Reserve — the same reserve that's part of the reason you're buying here in the first place. There's structurally nowhere for the road to expand into. The traffic you see on launch day is close to the traffic you'll have in year ten.

Then there's Thomson Plaza, right across the road — the same stretch where that Grab-pickup friction from Cluster A is at its worst. Four hundred and thirteen parking lots. [PUNCH: "413 carpark lots"] On weekends, that carpark genuinely fills up — and it keeps letting cars queue in anyway, which just backs up traffic at the entrance. Yes, you can walk instead. But try walking back with two bags of groceries in the Singapore sun, across a site that already has some of the longest internal distances of any new launch this year.

### Cluster D — The marketing claims that are only half true

This is the one I most want you to hear, because it directly affects the headline reason most people are buying this project.

"MRT at your doorstep." True — if you're in the right block. Blocks 5 and 7, the western cluster, sit up to 240 metres from the blocks closest to the MRT gateway. [PUNCH: "up to 240m from the near-MRT blocks"] If you buy there because the marketing told you MRT access is two minutes away, and your actual block is on the far side of the site, that promise quietly stops being true for you.

Same pattern with facilities. I went and checked which block sits furthest from the main pool and clubhouse. It's the same block that, on every other measure, is actually one of the best positioned on the entire site — most isolated, quietest, no block facing directly into it. [ENGAGE: "Would you trade facility distance for privacy and quiet? Or the other way round? Tell me which one you'd pick."] There is no stack on this site that wins on everything. Every single choice is a trade.

And then the one almost every review site now independently flags: west-facing units. Afternoon sun, roughly 2 to 7pm, real heat load, real aircon cost. [PUNCH: "West-facing: ~2–7pm heat exposure"] I actually flagged this one myself, live, before any of these review sites existed. And once you actually run a compass over the site plan — not guess, actually run it — the honest finding is bigger than "some units face west." The majority of stacks on this site pick up some degree of west sun. Some of them dead-on. Others are west-of-north or west-of-south, which sounds better on a floor plan, but the afternoon heat still comes in, just a little later and a little less brutal. [ENGAGE: "If you're currently eyeing a 'mostly west' stack here, tell me which block — I'll tell you honestly how bad the afternoon sun actually is."] If unblocked views are what's pulling you toward this site, go in knowing exactly which kind of sun exposure you're signing up for, because for most stacks here, it's not a maybe.

### Cluster E — The one nobody wants to say out loud

Okay. This next one, I want to be careful with, because it's not about anyone's beliefs. I'm not here to tell you what to believe, and I'm not going to make a joke out of it either. I'm going to state it as a fact, because it's one you deserve to know before you commit $2 to $3 million.

Bright Hill Drive — the same road Thomson Reserve sits on — is also home to Kong Meng San Phor Kark See Monastery. Singapore's largest Buddhist monastery. [PUNCH: "Same road: Bright Hill Temple"] It also operates one of only two private crematoria in the whole country, with a columbarium holding over 200,000 niches.

Now here's why I'm bringing this up, and it's not about whether you personally mind. For a meaningful segment of buyers in this market — and you know this if you've been in property as long as I have — proximity to a crematorium or columbarium is a genuine, deal-breaking concern. Feng shui, personal belief, family pressure, whatever the reason. It doesn't matter if you don't share that view. What matters is that a portion of your future buyer pool will. [ENGAGE: "Would this factor into your decision — either for yourself, or because of how it might affect your future buyer pool? Be honest in the comments."] That's not a spiritual question. That's a liquidity question. And nobody selling you a unit today is going to bring it up, because it's awkward and it doesn't help them close. I'd rather you hear it from me, now, than discover it from a buyer who walks away from your unit in eight years for exactly this reason.

### Cluster F — What's actually happening on this site, and around it, between now and your TOP

Two more, and these have nothing to do with the showflat. They're about timing.

First — the Cross Island Line. I mentioned earlier that Bright Hill is getting a second MRT interchange in the future. Here's the part that doesn't make it into the hype: as of right now, Bright Hill is an active Cross Island Line construction site, and LTA's own public timeline has Phase 1 completing around 2030. [PUNCH: "CRL Phase 1 — completing ~2030"] That is close to the same window as Thomson Reserve's own TOP. There's recent precedent for what living next to this kind of works actually sounds like — LTA has had to hand out noise blankets to residents near other Cross Island Line sites because the drilling genuinely keeps people up at night. I am not telling you this will happen here at the same intensity. I'm telling you it's an active worksite, on your own road, for a meaningful stretch of your first years of ownership — and that future interchange I had you hold onto earlier? You may be living next to the construction for it before you ever get to use it.

Second — and if you caught Part 2 of this series, you already know the internal version of this point: 1,268 units, 84% in one unit-type collection, competing with your own neighbours at resale. [PUNCH: "1,268 units, one TOP window"] What I haven't told you yet is the corridor-wide version. Thomson Reserve isn't the only mega-project racing toward TOP in 2030 and 2031. Other large launches in the same District 20/26 corridor — Lentor Gardens Residences among them — are landing TOP in that same window. [PUNCH: "Multiple mega-launches, same TOP window"] So it's not just your own 1,268 units hitting the resale market together. It's your entire corridor getting a wave of fresh, competing supply at roughly the same time. That doesn't make the project wrong. It makes your exit timing a real variable you need to plan for, not assume away.

## SCENE 5 — THE FLIP (17:00–22:00)

[PUNCH: "None of this is a reason to walk away."]

Here's the thing I want you to really sit with. I just gave you fifteen uncomfortable truths. Not one of them is a reason to avoid Thomson Reserve.

Almost every single one of them is a reason to be precise — about which stack you buy, and about when you plan your exit.

The entrance distance and the Grab-gate problem? That tells you to weigh blocks near the main gateway higher if daily convenience matters more to you than space or price. The lift math? That tells you to ask, stack by stack, how many units actually share each lift bank, not just assume every block behaves the same. The traffic pattern? That tells you your actual commute time depends on which gate you're closest to, not just which MRT line is nearest on a map. The MRT-distance and facilities-distance trade-off? That tells you no unit on this site is free of compromise — so the question isn't "is there a trade-off," it's "which trade-off can you actually live with."

The temple proximity is a bit different, and I want to treat it differently. I can't flip that one into "pick the right stack" — distance from Bright Hill Drive doesn't change block to block by much on a site this size. What it does do is tell you to be honest with yourself, and with whoever you're buying with, about whether it genuinely matters to you or your family — before you're three years into ownership and it becomes somebody else's problem to solve at resale. [ENGAGE: "If this is a factor for you, better to know that today than discover it on your own resale day — agree?"]

And the construction timeline and the corridor-wide TOP glut? Those flip into a planning question, not a stack question. If you know going in that you might have active MRT works nearby for part of your ownership, and that your resale window will land in a crowded corridor, you plan your holding period around that — you don't get blindsided by it in year three asking why the exit isn't moving as fast as you expected.

This is genuinely the difference between buying off a sales deck and buying off a site walk, and a proper read of the timeline. The project isn't the problem. Buying blind is the problem. [ENGAGE: "Which of these fifteen would actually change your mind on which block, or when, you'd buy? Tell me below."]

And look — I want to be straight with you about something else. Most of the physical issues I just walked you through? They exist, in some form, at basically every mega-development in Singapore. Big site, long walk, lift math, traffic at peak hour — that's the trade you make for the facilities, the greenery, and the scale that a smaller boutique project simply cannot give you. This isn't a uniquely-Thomson-Reserve problem. It's a mega-development problem, and Thomson Reserve happens to be an unusually good version of that trade, if you buy it with your eyes open — and with a plan for the two things, the temple proximity and the corridor-wide timing, that aren't really about the project at all.

## SCENE 6 — THE SCORECARD (22:00–24:30)

So let me give you my actual verdict, the same way I run every project through our method — not gut feel, a structured read against land, expenses, profit potential, and the benchmark, right through to what your realistic exit looks like.

[PUNCH: "WHAT WORKS"]

**What works.** Genuine MRT proximity for the right blocks. A school catchment you cannot manufacture. An unblockable nature reserve frontage. Resort-grade facilities at a scale few projects can match. On fundamentals, this clears the bar.

[PUNCH: "WHY I PAUSE"]

**Why I pause.** The site is big enough that "which block" matters more here than on almost any other launch I've reviewed this year. The marketing headline — MRT at your door — is only fully true for part of the development. The daily friction I walked you through, the jams, the lift math, the gate problem, is real and it is not going away, because there's structurally nowhere for the roads to expand. There's an active construction site on the same road for a meaningful stretch of your early ownership. Your resale window lands in a crowded corridor, not just a crowded project. And for a real segment of buyers, the temple proximity will matter — whether it matters to you or not, it will matter to someone in your future buyer pool.

None of that makes this the wrong project. It makes it a project where the stack you choose, and the plan you walk in with, matters more than almost anywhere else I've reviewed. Buy the wrong stack here, with no plan for timing, and you've bought all fifteen of today's problems with none of the upside. Buy the right one, with your eyes open on when you're likely to exit, and you've bought the reserve frontage, the MRT, the school, and the facilities — with the friction landing on someone else's block, not yours.
