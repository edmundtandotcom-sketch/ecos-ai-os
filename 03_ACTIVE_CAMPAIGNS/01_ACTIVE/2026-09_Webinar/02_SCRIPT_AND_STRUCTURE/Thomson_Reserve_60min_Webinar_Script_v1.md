## THE JOB THIS WEBINAR DOES — READ THIS FIRST

Edmund's own framing, direct from the working session: this is not an
information session. The YouTube videos already carry the information —
the numbers, the comparables, the walk-away math. If this webinar repeats
that, two things go wrong: people who've already watched the videos get
bored and leave, and everyone else gets everything they need for free and
has no reason to book a call.

**What actually converts, per Caleb Sim's structure:**

1. **Objective → Buying Criteria → Does Thomson Reserve meet it → What else
   might fit better.** Teach them to think, not what to conclude.
2. **You are not selling Thomson Reserve. You're selling your buying
   methodology and your judgment.** The room should leave thinking "I
   should get Edmund to run this on MY situation" — not "great, I'll go
   ballot" and not "the video already told me everything."
3. **The goal is controlled uncertainty, not conviction.** If everyone in
   the room leaves sure Thomson Reserve is right for them, you've failed —
   they don't need you anymore. Cast real doubt, using real reasoning,
   so the honest next step is a conversation with you.
4. **This webinar's actual product is the experience of being coached.**
   Every teaching block should feel like you're doing for the room, live,
   what you'd do for a paying client — a taste, not the whole meal. That
   experience is what gets sold in the pitch.
5. **Pre-frame constantly, not just at the open.** Every section should
   remind the room, in different language, that this is what working with
   you looks like — "this is exactly the conversation I'd have with you
   privately, except I don't know your numbers yet."

Two conversion tracks close this webinar, both explicitly named in the
pitch: **(a) the Next Move Strategy Session** — for anyone whose situation
needs the full advisory conversation, and **(b) Thomson Reserve balloting
support** — for anyone specifically circling this launch and its
October preview. Same booking action, two doors in.

## 6:00 – 18:00 — TEACH 1: OBJECTIVE

**[SCREEN: "What's your objective?" — 4 archetypes appear one at a time]**

Before anyone can judge whether a project is "good," you need to answer
one question honestly: **what are you actually trying to do with this
purchase?** Not what sounds sensible. What's actually true for you. Four
objectives come through my desk over and over:

**1. Stay-for-free.** You're upgrading your own home, and the real goal is
"I want to live somewhere better, and I don't want it to cost me anything
net, over time." Sounds simple. It's actually the strictest objective on
this list, because "for free" has a real definition, which I'll give you
in a second.

**2. Pure investment.** You're not living in it. You're comparing this
purchase against every other place that money could go — including just
leaving it invested elsewhere. Completely different criteria from someone
buying to stay.

**3. Buying for the kids.** Very common in this market, and Thomson
Reserve is going to come up a lot in this conversation because of a
specific school nearby — I'll show you exactly why that matters, and
exactly where people get the details wrong.

**4. Legacy / multi-generation net worth.** You're not buying for the next
5 years — you're buying for the next 20 to 30, either holding it long-term
or eventually passing it down. This objective cares about things nobody
else on this list cares about — lease decay being the big one.

**[SCREEN: Poll — "Which one is closest to you right now?" 4 options]**

Answer honestly in the chat — 1 to 4. Not what sounds most sensible.
What's actually true for you.

**[Read 5–6 responses, comment on the mix]**

Here's why naming this matters more than people think. If your objective
is just "I want to stay somewhere," almost anything works — you could buy
a random resale unit and be fine. But if it's "I want to stay **for
free**," that's a completely different bar: the property needs to
appreciate enough to cover your total interest cost, your renovation
outlay, and still leave you real growth on top. I use a benchmark of at
least 3 to 4% a year as the floor for that to be true. Below that, you're
not staying for free — you're paying rent to yourself and calling it
something nicer.

Same logic for investment — if you're comparing this to doing nothing with
the money, your real benchmark isn't "did the price go up," it's "did it
beat what doing nothing would have made you," after every extra cost a
property carries that a passive investment doesn't.

And for the kids' objective — the number that matters isn't "is there a
good school nearby." It's whether you're inside a specific priority
registration radius, because that's the difference between a real
advantage and a nice-to-have.

This is the part most sessions like this skip entirely — they jump
straight to "here's why the project is great" without ever asking what
you're actually trying to achieve. **Once you know your objective, your
criteria stop being vague and become something you can actually check a
project against.** That's next.

## 18:00 – 33:00 — TEACH 2: BUYING CRITERIA (THE LIVE WORKSHEET)

**[SCREEN: "Grab a pen — we're building your Buying Criteria Worksheet,
live." Build a clean 6-row table on screen as you go.]**

This is the part most people never get from an agent, because most agents
don't actually have one — they have a project they're trying to move, and
they reverse-engineer reasons why it fits you. I'm going to build the real
one with you, live, right now. Six criteria. Write these down — you can
use this on Thomson Reserve today, or on literally any launch after this.

**1. Location fundamentals.** Not "is it near an MRT" — how many lines,
how many minutes, and is there a future line coming. One stop from an
interchange is a different asset than a station on a dead-end line.

**2. Land cost basis versus what's selling nearby right now.** The one
almost nobody checks, and one of the most important. What did the
developer pay for the land, per square foot of buildable area, compared
to what land is trading for in nearby sites today. Lower land cost gives
the developer room to price competitively — and gives you more margin for
error if the market softens later. High land cost means the launch price
has almost no room to move.

**3. Developer track record — specifically their last two launches' actual
take-up and resale performance.** Not "is it a big name." A big name can
still misprice a project. What matters is how their last comparable
launch actually sold, and how it's performed since.

**4. Exit liquidity — how many resale transactions happen in the
surrounding district each year.** The most-skipped criterion, and the one
that bites hardest, years later. A location can be objectively excellent —
great school, great MRT — and still be a thin resale market, meaning very
few transactions a year. Thin volume means when you eventually want to
sell, you could be waiting a long time, with very little pricing power.

**5. Allocation odds relative to demand.** How many units exist in the
type you actually want, versus how many people are expected to chase them.
A hyped launch with a small run of your preferred unit type can mean your
odds of even getting it are low — this is the one that decides your actual
ballot strategy, not just whether you like the project.

**6. Rentability and future supply pipeline** — for the investment
objective specifically. What else is being built nearby that will compete
with you for tenants in the next five years. A great rental story today can
get diluted fast by a wave of new completions.

**[SCREEN: full 6-row worksheet, clean, left up 10–15 sec]**

That's the tool. Depending on your objective from ten minutes ago, some of
these matter enormously and some barely matter at all. If you're staying
for 20 years, exit liquidity matters far less to you than to an investor.
If you're buying for the kids, allocation odds might be the single most
important row on this whole sheet — because it doesn't matter how good the
project is if you can't actually secure the unit type you need.

This is genuinely the same worksheet I sit down with every client and
build together before they even think about a showflat. You've just done
step one of that process, for free, in the last fifteen minutes.

Now — let's run Thomson Reserve through it. Live. Honestly.

## 33:00 – 48:00 — TEACH 3: DOES THOMSON RESERVE MEET THE CRITERIA?

**[SCREEN: Thomson Reserve — quick fact strip, kept brief on purpose]**

Quick shared facts so we're working from the same page — I'll keep this
tight, because if you've seen my other videos you already know this part.
District 20, Bright Hill Drive. 99-year leasehold. 1,268 units across six
towers — a joint venture of three established Singapore developers. Direct
sheltered link to Upper Thomson MRT. A well-known school inside 1km.
Previewing later this year. **No official price has been released.**

Now let's go criterion by criterion, and I'll be straight with you on
every single one.

**Criteria 1 — Location fundamentals.** This is genuinely where it's
strongest. Direct MRT link, multiple line connections reachable without
transfers, a future line already planned nearby. On raw connectivity, this
scores near the top of anything I've reviewed this year. **Strong pass.**

**Criteria 2 — Land cost basis.** The land was acquired at a level that
sits below what recent nearby land sales have been going for. That's
genuinely a good sign — it means there's room for the developer to price
competitively, which historically means some margin protection for early
buyers too. **Pass** — and this is the number that actually tells you
whether the eventual launch price has real room in it, not just marketing
copy calling it "attractively priced."

**Criteria 3 — Developer track record.** The consortium behind this has
decades of delivery between them, and their most recent comparable
launches have held up well on resale. **Pass**, with one honest caveat — a
three-way joint venture can sometimes move slower on things like show
unit finishing and defect resolution than a single strong developer does.
Not a dealbreaker. Just something to know going in.

**Criteria 4 — Exit liquidity.** Here's where I have to be honest rather
than confident: **I can't fully grade this yet.** The project hasn't
launched, so there's no resale history for it specifically. The district's
broader resale pattern has generally been healthy, but the real test only
shows up years after completion — and that gap, between "the location
looks great today" and "the resale market actually performs later," is
genuinely the biggest trap I see buyers fall into across every launch I
review, not just this one. **Verdict: unproven — and "I don't know yet" is
the honest answer, which is exactly why this is the criterion worth
getting a second opinion on before you commit, not after.**

**Criteria 5 — Allocation odds.** Based on what's public so far, the bulk
of the unit count sits in the 2- and 3-bedroom range, with a smaller run
of larger units. If your objective points you toward a larger unit — a
family buying for the long term, or the legacy objective from earlier —
you're competing for a meaningfully smaller slice of total supply on a
project everyone's currently talking about. **Verdict: depends entirely on
your unit type** — this is exactly the kind of number that should shape
your ballot strategy before preview day, not something you discover
standing in the showflat.

**Criteria 6 — Rentability / future supply.** For the investment-objective
folks — this is a large single release. Great for facilities and lower
per-unit maintenance, but also means a lot of similar stock could hit the
resale and rental market around the same completion window, which can
soften rental rates in that specific window. Worth modelling, not a red
flag on its own.

**[SCREEN: school-priority callout, brief]**

One thing specifically for anyone who answered "kids" on the earlier
poll — the nearby school does sit inside a genuine priority-registration
radius, and that's a real, defensible advantage. But eligibility is
measured from the specific unit address, and the rules can shift between
now and your child's actual registration year. If that's your main reason
for looking at this project, that's exactly the kind of detail worth
verifying against your own timeline before you ballot, not after.

**[SCREEN: scorecard recap — 6 rows, verdicts filled in]**

So here's the honest scoreboard: strong on location, strong on land
economics, solid developer backing, a genuinely good story for the
school-priority objective if your timeline lines up — and two real,
unresolved questions: exit liquidity we can't test for years, and
allocation odds that swing hard depending on your unit type.

**That's not a "yes, buy it," and it's not a "no, avoid it." It's "it
depends entirely on your objective, your unit type, and your timeline" —
which is exactly the answer an honest process is supposed to give you.**

## 48:00 – 60:00 — TEACH 4: WHAT ELSE MIGHT FIT BETTER?

**[SCREEN: "Thomson Reserve isn't launching in a vacuum." — 2-3 other live

**[SCREEN: brief comparable strip]**

Depending on your objective, there are other live launches I've already

objective is the strongest possible exit liquidity because you know you'll

Reserve wins on every criterion for every objective — because it doesn't,
and any agent telling you it does is selling you something, not advising
you.

**[SCREEN: "It's not Thomson Reserve vs nothing. It's Thomson Reserve vs
your actual alternatives." — hold]**

This is genuinely the point of today. If you leave this session thinking
"okay, Thomson Reserve is clearly the answer," I haven't done my job.
The honest state most of you should be in right now is: **it might be
right for you, it might not, and there are at least one or two other
things worth putting on the table before you commit anything.** That
uncertainty isn't a failure of this session — it's the correct output of
an honest process. Certainty this early is usually a sign someone skipped
a step, not that they found the answer.
