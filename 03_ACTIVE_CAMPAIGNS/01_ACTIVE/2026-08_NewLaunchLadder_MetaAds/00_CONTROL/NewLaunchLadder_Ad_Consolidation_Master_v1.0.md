## 0. Governing rule (inherited)

Inherited from `Second_Property_Ladder_Canonical_Angle_Control_Register_v1.0.md`:

> An angle is not a headline and not a script format. Each angle must own **one** primary audience moment, **one** hidden cause, **one** belief to overturn, **one** distinct reframe, and **one** bridge into the framework. Narrator, story format and length are **formats beneath the angle** and must never quietly introduce a second angle.

**This was the exact failure in the Ads folder.** Seven documents were carrying two ideas. Three documents (the Likes/Dislike trio) shared 70–80% of their body text and differed only by narrator — that is one angle in three formats, not three angles. **Namespace:** this campaign uses `NL01`–`NL06`. It is a **separate register** from Second Property Ladder `A01–A20` / `B01–B20`. Different framework (New Launch Ladder™), different ICP (2nd/3rd/4th-property new-launch investor), different funnel. Do not cross-reference IDs between the two registers.

### The retrofit rule — apply to every ad

Every existing ad argues that **return on equity** is the number that matters, then proves it with **purchase price and sale price** — which is return on investment. The argument dismantles itself.

**From v1.0 onward, no proof point ships without two figures: capital committed, and annualised return on that capital.** "$1.15M to $1.525M" is not proof. "$321K committed, ~22.9% a year" is proof. This is the single largest quality gain available and it costs nothing.

## 5. NL02 — THE 5-POINT POLICY *(new)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Audience moment | Has been told "confirm can buy" by every agent they've spoken to |
| Hidden cause | Nobody advising them has a stated standard, so every project passes |
| Belief attacked | "A good agent finds you a good project" |
| Reframe | A good adviser is defined by what they refuse to sell you |
| Bridge | The Ladder is the filter that does the refusing |

**Why this exists.** This line was sitting as Hook 7 of 10 inside `S&P AD` and has never run:

> *"If a property can't beat the S&P 500 by at least 5%, we don't pitch it."*

That is a **policy**, not a claim. A policy is the most persuasive thing a salesperson can own, because it is the only statement in the category that costs the seller money. Every competitor says what they will sell you. Nobody says what they refuse to.

### Hooks

**NL02-H1 — The Policy**
> "If a project can't beat the S&P 500 by at least 5 points, we don't pitch it. That rule costs me deals every single launch."

**NL02-H2 — What I Refuse To Sell**
> "Every agent will tell you what they can sell you. Ask one what they refuse to sell you — and watch the silence."

**NL02-H3 — Ask For The Number**
> "Ask your agent what return this project needs to hit before they'd tell you not to buy it. If there's no number, there's no standard."

**NL02-H4 — Confirm Can Buy**
> "'Confirm can buy.' 'Place a cheque first.' 'Every new launch also can make money.' Of course they say that. Nobody gets paid to tell you to skip one."

**NL02-H5 — The Deals I Don't Do**
> "The last launch everybody was balloting for, I told my clients to skip. Here's the rule that made me say it."

### Body — "The Rule That Costs Me Deals"

> Here's a rule that costs me money.
>
> If a project can't beat the S&P 500 by at least 5 points, we don't pitch it.
>
> Not "we mention the risks." Not "we let the client decide."
>
> We don't pitch it.
>
> And I'll be honest with you about why that's uncomfortable. Every launch season, that rule takes projects off my own table. Projects that would have paid me. Projects that were selling well. Projects where the queue was around the block and every other agent in Singapore was posting about the take-up rate.
>
> So why hold to it?
>
> Because of what happens when you don't have a rule.
>
> Without a standard, **every project passes.** There's always a reason it could work. Good location. Good developer. Good timing. Limited units. Transformation coming. You can build a case for anything if you're not measuring it against something.
>
> And that's why you've probably never heard an agent tell you to skip a launch. It isn't that they're dishonest. It's that they have no number to fail against.
>
> So here's mine.
>
> Your money has an alternative. It can sit in the S&P 500 and compound at roughly 10% a year, with no mortgage, no stamp duty, no interest, no agent fees and no four-year wait.
>
> So if you're going to commit $500K, $700K, sometimes $1M of cash and CPF, take on 25 years of debt, and give up your liquidity for four years — the return has to clearly beat the thing you could have done instead. Not match it. Beat it, by enough that the risk and the illiquidity were worth taking.
>
> I hold my own money to it. $321K committed on one, out three years later at roughly 22.9% a year after every cost. $292K on another, about 12.9% a year, on a property that itself only grew 5% annually.
>
> `[CS-1 — MATCH: a specific project Edmund refused to pitch because it failed the hurdle, and what has happened to it since. Needs the project, the number it needed to clear, the number it projected, and the outcome. This is the case study that makes the policy real instead of rhetorical.]`
>
> That's what the **New Launch Ladder™** actually is. It isn't a shortlist of projects I like.
>
> It's the filter that disqualifies most of them.
>
> We work backwards from your exit. What capital goes in. What the realistic resale price is four to six years out. What financing and transaction costs take. What return on equity is left. And whether that clears the hurdle by enough.
>
> If it doesn't, we walk. Even if the showflat is packed. Even if everyone is balloting. Even if it's the hottest launch that month.
>
> So before you place another cheque, ask the person advising you one question:
>
> **"What return would this project have to fail to hit, before you told me not to buy it?"**
>
> If they don't have a number, they don't have a standard. And without a standard, they were always going to say yes.
>
> Click the link below. Tell me which project you're looking at, and I'll run it against the rule — including telling you if it fails.
>
> **You are one property away.**

### Cold cutdown — 30 seconds

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Audience moment | Actively evaluating one named launch, about to ballot |
| Hidden cause | They're assessing the *project* when the money is decided by the *unit, price and exit* |
| Belief attacked | "Good project equals good buy" |
| Reframe | I can like the project and still tell you to walk away from your unit |
| Bridge | The Four Doubts, then the walkaway price |
| Absorbs | All three Likes/Dislike documents |

**Structural ruling.** These were never three angles. They shared 70–80% of their body text and differed only by who was narrating. From v1.0, **there is one body.** The narrator is an intro that swaps. Running near-duplicate bodies concurrently splits Meta's learning and suppresses delivery.

### Narrator intros — these are formats, not angles

**Variant A — The Analyst** *(default; strongest hook in the entire folder)*
> "I've bought 4 new launches for myself and my family in recent years. Here's what I like about [PROJECT] — and what would make me walk away."
> I'm not reviewing the brochure. I'm looking at the entry, the future buyer, the competition and the exit.

**Variant B — The Daughter** *(highest emotional proof; converts criticism into care)*
> "My daughter already benefited from Parc Clematis — in at $1.15M on about $321K of capital, out at $1.525M, roughly 22.9% a year. Now I'm looking at [PROJECT] for her next move. Can it do the same job?"
> Because when it's your own family's money, you look very differently at the entry price, the future buyer and the exit.

**Variant C — Property #2** *(most on-ICP)*
> "If [PROJECT] is going to be your second property, don't ask whether it's a good project. Ask whether it makes your overall position stronger."
> Property #2 is where bad decisions get expensive. More capital, more debt, and a bet that this one actually moves you closer to Property #3.

**Rule:** the narrator changes the intro and the final two lines only. **The Four Doubts body is identical across all three.** Never run more than one variant per ad set.

### THE FOUR DOUBTS — reusable generator

*This is the productisation gap. The Amberwood analysis was excellent but lived in Edmund's head, so every new project meant thinking from scratch. This turns it into fill-in-the-blanks. Answer these four for any launch and the ad writes itself.*

**Selection rule:** use the 3–4 doubts that are genuinely **true** for the project. Never force all four. A forced doubt reads as negging and destroys the credibility the whole format depends on.

**Opening rule:** always lead with at least one genuine **like**, stated specifically. The format only works because the praise is real.

---

**DOUBT 1 — WHO ELSE IS COMING?** *(supply and competition)*

*Inputs:* remaining plots in the precinct, total planned homes, GLS pipeline, launches expected between now and year 4–6
*Sources:* URA GLS programme, precinct masterplan, developer launch pipeline

> Everyone's talking about [PROJECT] being an early mover in [PRECINCT]. That can be good. But you're not buying one isolated project — [PRECINCT] is planned for up to [X] homes across [Y] plots. So when you want to sell in [YEAR], do those newer launches push your price up? Or does your buyer simply choose the newer project next door?

---

**DOUBT 2 — WHO BUYS IT FROM YOU?** *(future buyer pool)*

*Inputs:* unit mix, smallest unit type available, quantum band at your exit price, likely buyer profile

> [PROJECT] has [zero 1-bedders and zero 2-bedders / X% small units]. Everything is [N]-bedroom and above. I actually like the [family / investor] positioning. But it also means your future buyer pool becomes more specific. At a $[Q]M-plus quantum, who exactly is buying from you next — a family, an upgrader, a multi-generation household? And will there be enough of them at the price you need?

---

**DOUBT 3 — WHO SETS YOUR PRICE?** *(resale liquidity and price discovery)*

*Inputs:* total units, expected annual resale transactions, resale-benchmark behaviour in comparable small projects

> [N] units feels boutique. Nice. But when you exit, fewer transactions also means fewer resale benchmarks. The first few owners who sell will heavily influence where everybody else gets priced. Do you want your exit price set by whoever happens to be most desperate that year?

---

**DOUBT 4 — HOW MUCH ROOM IS LEFT?** *(entry price versus precinct ceiling)*

*Inputs:* launch psf, surrounding resale psf, the gap, comparable prestigious-address projects that underperformed anyway

> And don't get hypnotised by "[DISTRICT]". Nearby projects prove a prestigious postcode alone doesn't guarantee growth. If you're entering around $[X] psf while surrounding resale sits at $[Y] psf, that gap has to close before you make anything. So the question stops being "is [PROJECT] a good project?" and becomes: **at this price, which unit still has enough room?**

## 7. NL04 — PROPERTY #2 HAS A JOB *(new)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Audience moment | Owns one property, has capital, shopping for a second |
| Hidden cause | They evaluate Property #2 in isolation instead of against what they already hold |
| Belief attacked | "Two properties is more wealth than one" |
| Reframe | Property #2 either complements Property #1 or it doubles the same bet |
| Bridge | The Ladder defines #2's job before comparing projects |

**Why this exists.** The `Likes/Dislike (2nd Property)` doc raised "does it complement what you already own?" and then never answered it — it promised portfolio thinking and delivered the same project analysis as the other two. That unanswered question is a whole angle, and nobody in this market is writing it.

### Hooks

**NL04-H1 — Expensive Mistakes**
> "Property #2 is where bad decisions get expensive. More capital, more debt, and a bet that this one actually moves you closer to Property #3."

**NL04-H2 — One Job**
> "Your second property has exactly one job: grow your equity and create your next move. So does the one you're looking at actually do that?"

**NL04-H3 — Not A Strategy**
> "Buying a second property just to say you own two properties is not a strategy. It's a second mortgage with a nicer story attached."

**NL04-H4 — The Same Bet Twice**
> "If your second property is in the same segment, at the same quantum, exiting to the same buyer as your first — you didn't diversify. You doubled down and called it a portfolio."

**NL04-H5 — Both On The Market**
> "Picture the year you want to sell. Both your properties are on the market at the same time, in the same segment, competing for the same buyer. You just became your own competition."

### Body — "The Same Bet Twice"

> If you already own one property and you're looking at your second, everyone will tell you the same thing.
>
> "Two properties better than one."
>
> Maybe. But here's what nobody checks.
>
> Most second properties aren't a second position. They're **the same bet, placed twice.**
>
> Same segment. Similar quantum. Similar unit type. Similar hold period. Exiting to the same kind of buyer, in the same kind of market.
>
> And when that's true, you haven't built a portfolio. You've doubled your exposure to one outcome — and taken on a second mortgage to do it.
>
> So what does "complement" actually mean? It's four things, and they're all testable before you buy.
>
> **One — a different buyer at exit.** If both properties sell to the same profile at the same quantum, then in a soft market for that profile, both of your assets go quiet at once. Property #2 should be able to find a buyer in a year when Property #1 can't.
>
> **Two — a staggered exit window.** If both properties want to be sold in the same 12 months, you'll be forced to accept a weak price on one of them. Your second property's realistic exit should sit deliberately ahead of or behind your first.
>
> **Three — a different job.** One of them is probably holding value and giving you somewhere to live. Then the other one's job is to **grow equity fast enough to fund Property #3.** If both are being asked to do both jobs, neither is being optimised for either.
>
> **Four — it must leave you able to move again.** This is the one that gets missed most. After Property #2 completes, how much cash is left? How much borrowing capacity? Whose name is still free? A second property that consumes every resource you have hasn't advanced your position — it's ended your sequence. You own more and you can do less.
>
> `[CS-2 — MATCH: two clients side by side. One whose Property #2 duplicated Property #1 and who ended up with both assets stuck in the same market at the same time. One whose #2 complemented, exited on schedule, and funded #3. I need segment, quantum, timing and outcome for both. The contrast is the entire ad — a single positive case is much weaker here.]`
>
> That's why we don't start by asking which project you like.
>
> We start with what you already own. What Property #1 is doing, what it's worth, who buys it, and when it should be released.
>
> Then we define what Property #2's job is. And only then do we look at projects — because now there's something to measure them against.
>
> That's the **New Launch Ladder™**. We map your existing position, define the job, filter launches against it, set the walkaway price, and check the move still leaves you able to make Property Move #3.
>
> Because the goal was never to own two properties.
>
> It's for #2 to fund #3, and #3 to fund #4.
>
> So before you commit to your second property, don't ask "is this a good project?"
>
> Ask: **"does this make my overall position stronger — or does it just give me another mortgage?"**
>
> Click the link below. Tell me what you already own and what you're looking at, and we'll work out whether it strengthens your ladder or just adds a rung that goes nowhere.
>
> **You are one property away.**
